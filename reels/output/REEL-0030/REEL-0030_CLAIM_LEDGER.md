# Reel 0030 Claim Ledger

| Narration unit | Evidence label | Evidence basis | Required boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| High-curiosity trivia answers were remembered better in reviewed laboratory studies. | Empirical pattern summarized in review | PACE review describes immediate and delayed memory findings in trivia paradigms. [1] | Use laboratory/task wording; no academic-performance claim. |
| Incidental faces shown during high-curiosity anticipation were better recognized in some studies. | Empirical pattern summarized in review | PACE review summarizes incidental-face recognition findings. [1] | Say “कुछ अध्ययनों में”; no general spillover guarantee. |
| PACE connects prediction, appraisal, curiosity, exploration, and memory as a framework. | Proposed framework | PACE article explicitly presents testable predictions. [1] | Name it as a framework, not an established individual brain mechanism. |
| Curiosity has no single agreed definition and measures differ. | Review boundary | Kidd & Hayden discuss definitional and task-standardization limits. [2] | Do not infer intrinsic curiosity from a click, search, rating, or gaze alone. |
| Confidence and perceived importance moderated curiosity in two experiments. | Empirical finding | Spitzer et al. reported task-specific replicated/moderated patterns. [3] | No ideal certainty level or universal question-design formula. |
| Understanding, repetition, feedback, sleep, and context influence learning outcomes. | Editorial boundary | General learning factors are named only to reject a one-factor causal story. | Do not quantify or claim their effects for the viewer. |

## Prohibited claims

Do not claim that curiosity guarantees memory, exam marks, motivation, productivity, mental-health benefit, or learning in every setting. Do not call PACE a proof that curiosity releases dopamine on command, attach a brain-region explanation to an individual, present curiosity as manipulation, or use any unverified statistic.
