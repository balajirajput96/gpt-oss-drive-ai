# Reel 0029 Claim Ledger

| Narration unit | Evidence label | Evidence basis | Required boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| A partly known but unresolved question can sometimes evoke curiosity. | Evidence-bounded empirical framing | Information-gap account summarized by Kidd & Hayden; trivia-task findings. [1] | Use “कभी” and do not promise a response. |
| Information-gap account describes a perceived difference between current and desired knowledge. | Theory/account | Loewenstein’s account; reviewed by Kidd & Hayden. [1] [3] | Call it an “account,” not a proven universal mechanism. |
| Trivia-task curiosity was often highest at intermediate confidence. | Empirical finding | Kang et al. finding summarized in Kidd & Hayden; replicated/extended by Spitzer et al. [1] [2] | Restrict to task paradigms; no numeric cutoff. |
| Perceived importance moderated the confidence–curiosity relation in a later study. | Empirical finding | Spitzer et al. two-experiment study. [2] | Avoid claiming importance guarantees learning or engagement. |
| Mystery alone may be insufficient; novelty, value, context, and incentives can matter. | Review/model boundary | Kidd & Hayden review; Dubey & Griffiths model. [1] [4] | Do not reduce curiosity to one variable. |
| Clicking can have non-curiosity motives. | Conceptual/review boundary | Curiosity and information seeking have overlapping but non-identical definitions. [1] | Do not infer intrinsic motivation from behavior alone. |

## Prohibited claims

No line may state that an information gap reliably manipulates attention, makes every learner study, improves memory, triggers dopamine on demand, diagnoses a condition, or has a universally ideal size. No unsupported percentage, engagement statistic, spiritual claim, or neural-causation claim may be added.
