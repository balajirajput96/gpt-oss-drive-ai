# Reel_0020 research record — sleep, stress, and environment in attention

## Evidence source 1: acute stress meta-analysis
Grant S. Shields, Matthew A. Sazma, and Andrew P. Yonelinas, “The Effects of Acute Stress on Core Executive Functions: A Meta-Analysis and Comparison with Cortisol,” *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* (2016), DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2016.06.038; accessible via PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5003767/

The meta-analysis found that acute stress impaired working memory and cognitive flexibility, while effects on inhibition were nuanced. Moderators included variables such as sex, delay between stress and testing, task type, and study precision. The review explicitly describes inconsistencies and cases where stress may have no effect or may improve performance. Safe use: acute laboratory stress can shift executive performance under studied conditions; it is not evidence that every stressful moment uniformly destroys attention.

## Evidence source 2: sleep-deprivation meta-analysis
Chloe R. Newbury, Rebecca Crowley, Kathleen Rastle, and Jakke Tamminen, “Sleep Deprivation and Memory: Meta-Analytic Reviews of Studies on Sleep Deprivation Before and After Learning,” *Psychological Bulletin* (2021), DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000348; accessible via PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8893218/

The meta-analysis focused on total acute sleep deprivation of at least one full night, not every kind of partial or chronic sleep restriction. It found detrimental effects on memory for newly learned material both when deprivation occurred before learning and after learning, with moderators including immediate versus recovery-sleep testing and memory type. The paper also notes that attention and fatigue can affect immediate testing. Safe use: total acute sleep deprivation has measurable task-specific cognitive costs; do not generalize its effect size to all sleep patterns.

## Evidence source 3: environmental noise review anchor
Richard Thompson et al., “Noise pollution and human cognition: An updated systematic review and meta-analysis of recent evidence,” *Environment International* (2022), DOI/source record: https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34649047; publisher record: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0160412021005304

The review synthesizes epidemiological evidence on environmental noise and cognition. For the reel, use only cautious context language: noise exposure can be associated with cognitive outcomes in particular settings and populations, and mechanisms may involve distraction, stress, or sleep disruption. Do not claim that any specific decibel threshold guarantees an attention effect for every viewer.

## Safe synthesis
Sleep, acute stress, and environment are not three universal “attention switches.” Total acute sleep loss, a laboratory stressor, and a particular noise exposure can change performance under defined conditions, but effects vary by task, timing, population, and measurement. The reel should present these as evidence-bounded context factors, not diagnosis or a promise that changing one factor will fix attention.
