## Scope

The common misconception is that doing several things at once always means the mind is processing every part with equal attention, or that a person who switches frequently must be practising a single general “multitasking skill.” The research record instead distinguishes **task switching**—changing from one task-set or rule to another—from **dual-task performance**, in which two demands can overlap but may interfere depending on their processing requirements. A neuroscience overview notes that everyday multitasking often entails switching or leaving one task unfinished to do another; task-switch experiments compare switch trials with repeat trials. [1] [2]

This is not a claim that every paired activity fails, that a person has no agency, or that every interruption has the same size of effect. Task type, complexity, familiarity, preparation, measurement approach, and setting matter. The evidence record therefore prohibits dramatic brain, safety, intelligence, and diagnosis claims. [1] [2] [3]

## Allowed claims

The narration may say that everyday multitasking can involve rapid alternation between goals, rules, or information streams. It may explain with a neutral example: moving between writing a message and solving a problem can require re-establishing the relevant rule or goal. It may say that laboratory research often finds slower or less accurate performance after a task change than on repeated-task trials, while the size and meaning of that difference depend on the task. [1] [2] [4]

The narration may correct the misconception that a task-switch result is a diagnosis, intelligence score, or permanent personal label. It may say that research on media multitasking reports mixed or measure-dependent associations, so a person’s routine cannot be reduced to one laboratory finding. [3]

## Prohibited overclaims

The narration must not say that humans “cannot multitask,” that the brain literally handles only one thing, that multitasking permanently damages attention, that phone use causes a disorder, or that any viewer should self-diagnose. It must not give an exact time, percentage, cost, or ideal focus interval. It must not make safety or driving recommendations, productivity promises, or treatment advice. It must not borrow citations, claims, narration, visuals, audio, video, captions, or legacy assets from the historical `R0032_attention-span-myth-vs-evidence` package.

## Planned narrative boundary

The Hindi reel will start with the common claim **“मैं multitasking में बहुत अच्छा हूँ।”** It will clarify that in many cognitive tasks, what feels like doing two things at once can include quick switching between task rules. It will use the idea of “switch” versus “stay” trials as a scientific model, then name the boundary: some activities can overlap and costs vary, so this is not a verdict on a person. The final takeaway will be: **तेज़ी से काम बदलना और हर काम को एक साथ बराबर attention देना एक ही बात नहीं है।** This is an editorial synthesis of the cited sources, not a diagnostic or productivity claim.
