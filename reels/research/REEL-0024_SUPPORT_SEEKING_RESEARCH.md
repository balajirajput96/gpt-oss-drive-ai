# Reel 0024 Research Record — When Seeking Support May Be Appropriate

**Registry identity:** `0024 / Batch_001 / MND / L01 / Q24`
**Question:** कब सहायता लेना ठीक है?

## Initial authoritative public-health finding

NIMH’s 2025 public information guide frames the first consideration as the impact of symptoms on daily life. It distinguishes mild symptoms lasting under two weeks from severe symptoms lasting two weeks or more, and says to seek professional help for the latter. It also directs people who are experiencing immediate danger related to self-harm to emergency/crisis help. This is U.S. public-health guidance, not a diagnosis tool, a universal threshold, or a substitute for local emergency and health services. [1]

## Production boundary derived from this source

The reel may say that support need not be reserved for a particular label, and that persistent or severe distress that substantially affects day-to-day functioning is a reasonable reason to contact a qualified local professional. It may distinguish talking with a trusted person from professional assessment. It must not diagnose a viewer, claim a precise two-week rule applies everywhere, name U.S.-only numbers for a global Hindi audience, promise an outcome, or portray crisis support as a replacement for local emergency services.

If a future script mentions immediate safety concerns, it must use general language: **contact local emergency or crisis support now**. It must not provide individualized crisis triage or assume a country-specific service applies to every viewer.

## Peer-reviewed evidence on help-seeking barriers

Gulliver, Griffiths, and Christensen’s 2010 systematic review synthesized 22 published qualitative and quantitative studies about young people’s perceived barriers and facilitators to seeking help for common mental-health problems. Its abstract identifies stigma and embarrassment, difficulty recognizing symptoms, and preference for self-reliance as prominent perceived barriers. Positive prior experiences, social support, and encouragement from others appeared as possible facilitators. [2]

The scope is important: this review concerns adolescents and young adults, reflects study participants’ perceived barriers and facilitators, and includes research predominantly conducted in Australia and the United States. It cannot establish that any one barrier causes a particular person not to seek help, identify an individual’s need for care, or determine what local service is accessible or appropriate.

## Production boundary derived from the review

The reel may state that asking for support can be difficult because concerns such as embarrassment, privacy, trust, or self-reliance are commonly reported in this research. It may offer a non-clinical first step such as naming the concern to a trusted person and asking for help finding an appropriate local option. It must not pressure disclosure, assume family/friends are safe for everyone, treat informal support as equivalent to professional care, or claim that stigma is the sole or universal barrier.
