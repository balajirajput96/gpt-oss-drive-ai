## Scholarly evidence and ethical frameworks

### Autonomy, disclosed influence, and alignment with a person’s goals

Jacobs (2019) analyzes ethical concerns around persuasive technologies. The paper distinguishes influence that is disclosed and respects a person’s options from hidden influence that disrupts self-authorship, and argues that alignment with users’ interests and autonomy matter ethically. Its concrete examples concern health-related technologies and vulnerable people; this reel must not generalize those examples to diagnose viewers or make medical claims. [1]

### Attention as an ethical and justice-related concern

Hartford and Stein (2022) offer an ethical viewpoint on potential attentional harms and digital inequality. They emphasize that internet engagement can have worthwhile as well as harmful aspects and that no straightforward relationship follows from time online alone. This supports a cautious ethical question—whether a design makes its attention-capturing intent and choices visible—rather than a universal verdict that all attention-oriented design is manipulation. [2]

### Systematic review of ethical factors in persuasive technologies

Rahman and Adaji (2024) report a systematic literature review of 17 articles. Their abstract identifies autonomy, consent, privacy, transparency, trust, value alignment, fairness, and addictive-design concerns as factors influencing ethical perceptions across contexts. This is a literature synthesis about ethical factors, not an experiment proving a particular feature causes harm in an individual user. [3]

## Evidence-bounded practical synthesis

This reel may invite viewers to ask four questions of an attention-capturing design: **What is it trying to influence? Is that intent clear? Can a person decline or change the setting easily? Whose goal and data are served?** This is an ethical reflection tool derived from the frameworks above. It is not a clinical screen, a legal test, a claim of unlawful conduct, or proof of harmful intent.

## Prohibited overclaims

Do not say a platform is definitely manipulative, addictive, illegal, or harmful; do not diagnose “addiction,” claim universal loss of free will, or make health-treatment recommendations. Keep philosophical claims and evidence claims distinct.
