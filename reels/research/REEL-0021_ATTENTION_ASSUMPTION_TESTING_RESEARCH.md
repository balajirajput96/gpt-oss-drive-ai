## Evidence record

### Empirical finding: a single subjective answer may mix sensitivity and response bias

Nartker, Firestone, Egeth, and Phillips (2025) tested inattentional-blindness reports with follow-up forced-choice questions and trials in which the unexpected stimulus was absent. In their study design, an initial yes/no report did not by itself settle whether visual information was unavailable: participants who denied noticing could, at a group level, show above-chance sensitivity to tested features, while absent trials helped assess a conservative reporting criterion. This is **experiment-specific evidence** about that task design, not proof that any individual’s everyday perception is unreliable or that attention is unnecessary for awareness. [1]

### Systematic-review finding: self-report is informative but incomplete

Craig, Hale, Grainger, and Stewart (2020) conducted systematic reviews and meta-analyses of metacognitive self-report research. Their abstract concludes that self-reports can provide a useful overview of metacognitive knowledge and regulation, while the relation of particular self-report subscales to metacognitive behavior remains unclear. This supports treating a person’s report as one data source rather than a final measurement of performance. It does **not** mean self-report is useless or that one short self-check can measure a person’s overall metacognition. [2]

## Evidence-bounded practical synthesis

The following is a **practical learning heuristic**, not a clinical test and not a finding directly established by either study. To test a small attention-related assumption, a person can state a specific prediction before a low-stakes task, choose one observable outcome, record the result, compare it with a plausible alternative explanation, and repeat under a meaningfully different condition. For example, rather than conclude “I always notice everything,” write a narrower prediction such as “while counting a target, I will notice an unexpected change,” then compare the prediction with the observed result. The exercise can reveal a mismatch between expectation and this one task; it cannot diagnose attention, establish a stable trait, or decide a theory of consciousness.

## Prohibited overclaims

Do not claim that people always miss what is visible, that a yes/no report is false, that a home exercise measures awareness, that the result proves or disproves a theory of consciousness, or that any viewer has an attention disorder. Do not add numerical prevalence claims, medical advice, or fabricated citations.
